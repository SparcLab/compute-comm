1Elmore Family School of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Purdue University

Summary

We show that the wireless link determines where a wearable should run its neural network. Over Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), running the first layers on the wearable reduces energy for networks that compress their input early. Over Wi-R, a body-coupled link, streaming the raw sensor data uses the least energy for all six networks and 19 microcontrollers we model.

Wi-R couples the signal through the body instead of radiating it, which lowers the energy per transmitted bit by about 15× relative to BLE in our model. A split saves energy only when the transmission it removes costs more than the multiply-accumulate operations (MACs) it adds on the microcontroller (MCU).

Motivation

Wearables offload most of a modern neural network because they lack the memory and energy to run it. The largest of the 19 microcontrollers we evaluate has 4 MB of memory, whereas recent wearable foundation models exceed 100 million parameters. Most of the network therefore runs on a phone or an on-body hub.

The common design response is to compute part of the network on the wearable so that it transmits fewer bits. The reason is the cost of radios. Chatterjee et al. estimate that transmitting a bit costs about 104 times the energy of switching a bit in a 45–65 nm chip, and that BLE-class radios draw two to three orders of magnitude more power than sensing and computation [1]. Splitting a network and transmitting an intermediate feature map applies the same in-sensor analytics principle.

However, computation on a commercial microcontroller is not free: it costs tens to hundreds of picojoules per MAC, far more than switching a single bit. A split therefore saves energy only when the transmission it removes costs more than the computation it adds, and this comparison depends on the link. Figure 2 shows it for one network on one microcontroller.

Expressed in MACs, one bit transmitted over BLE costs the STM32U385 as much energy as 135 MACs, and one bit over Wi-R as much as 9, including the MCU time spent transmitting. SleepFM's tokenizer spends 89 MACs per bit it removes. The tokenizer therefore saves energy over BLE but not over Wi-R. The next section formalizes this comparison.

Break-even condition

We split the network after layer k. The wearable then computes Ck MACs and transmits Bk bits instead of the raw B0 bits. The split reduces energy when the following holds.

Step 1 · Energy comparison
Ck · eMAC  <  (B0 − Bk) · ēbit  +  Ck · Psl / Θ
The left side is the energy of the Ck MACs. The right side is the energy of the B0 − Bk bits no longer transmitted, plus the MCU sleep energy that the computation time displaces; Psl is the MCU's sleep power and Θ its MAC throughput in MACs per second, so Ck/Θ is the computation time.
Step 2 · Per-MAC form
eMAC  <  ēbit · (B0 − Bk) / Ck  +  Psl / Θ
Dividing by Ck expresses both sides in energy per MAC. The left side is what the microcontroller spends; the right side is the largest energy per MAC at which the split still saves energy.
Step 3 · Network and link terms
ρk = (B0 − Bk) / Ck   bits removed per MAC, a property of the network
ρk measures how efficiently the first k layers compress the input. Its inverse, 1/ρk, is easier to read: the number of MACs the network spends to remove one bit. SleepFM's tokenizer removes 12.72 Mbit with 1.13 billion MACs, so it spends 89 MACs per bit removed (ρ = 11.3 bits per 1,000 MACs). A network that needs many MACs to shrink its data slightly spends many MACs per bit, and a layer that does not shrink the data removes no bits at all.
ecrit = ēbit · ρk + Psl / Θ   the break-even energy per MAC
On-wearable computation reduces energy when eMAC < ecrit.
Step 4 · Cost of removing a bit against cost of sending it
1 / ρk  <  ēbit / (eMAC − Psl / Θ)
The same condition, rearranged. The left side is the number of MACs the network spends to remove one bit. The right side is the number of MACs whose energy equals one transmitted bit. Computing first reduces energy when removing a bit costs fewer MACs than sending it. On the STM32U385, one BLE bit is worth 135 MACs and one Wi-R bit 9; SleepFM's tokenizer spends 89, so it pays over BLE and not over Wi-R (Figure 3).
Per-bit link energy
ēbit = etx + (Ph − Psl − Plink) / R
ēbit is the all-in energy of one transmitted bit: radio energy, plus the MCU held awake during transmission, minus the MCU sleep and link keep-alive that the transmission time replaces.

Worked example: every number for SleepFM on the STM32U385

SleepFM reads 23 sleep-lab channels at 128 Hz in 5-min windows, which amounts to 14.13 Mbit of 16-bit samples. Its CNN tokenizer reduces them to 1.41 Mbit of 8-bit tokens with 1.13 GMAC, so ρ = 11.3 bits per 1,000 MACs. The STM32U385 spends 81 pJ per MAC (MLPerf Tiny measurement).

Robustness

We test each main result against parameter error and against the modeling choices most likely to change it.

ResultCondition under which it changes
Over Wi-R, raw streaming outperforms on-wearable computation in all 114 pairsThe result changes only if MCU compute energy is 3.9× lower than assumed or Wi-R energy per bit is 3.9× higher. It also requires a Wi-R rate of at least 2.5 Mb/s (at 1 Mb/s, one pair flips) and packet delivery of at least 99%. Because Wi-R has no acknowledgments, one pair flips at 95% delivery, two at 90% and four at 80%. Assumptions

All numbers are model estimates based on datasheets, Ixana's public power calculator [2] (screenshot below) and MLPerf Tiny benchmarks; we have not measured them on hardware. Ixana makes Wi-R, and one of the authors (S. Sen) founded it.

The numbers apply to the parameter values below and are not fixed properties of BLE, Wi-R or any MCU; the calculator recomputes them for any setting. The method carries over to other designs: compare the bits a split removes per MAC with the MCU's energy per MAC and the link's energy per bit.

What we count

  • Only the wearable's chip and radio, over one inference period
  • Not the receiving phone or hub. Phones have no Wi-R radio, so Wi-R needs a body-worn hub, and raw streaming moves all computation to it
  • Not the sensors or the model's accuracy
  • Every packet arrives (no losses)

The wearable

  • 8-bit (int8) network, 16-bit sensor data
  • Energy per MAC: measured by MLPerf Tiny where available, otherwise from the datasheet
  • Computes, then transmits; stays awake during transmission and sleeps otherwise

BLE

  • 2 Mb/s, one connection event every 30 ms, up to 6 packets per event
  • 244 bytes of data per packet
  • Stays connected between inferences: one empty connection event every 30 ms (about 160 µW)

Wi-R

  • 5 Mb/s, 255 bytes of data plus a 40-byte header per packet
  • 2.3 mW while sending, 3 µW asleep
  • No acknowledgments; sleeps between inferences

Settings

Data format

Bits per raw input value sets the size of the raw input (sensor samples, or per-minute features for LSM and SensorLM). The model number format sets the size of the weights, the working memory and every layer output that is sent. fp16 and fp32 also make compute slower and cost more energy than int8: ×2.2 on Cortex-M7, ×2.7 on M4/M33, ×20 on M0+ (no floating-point unit). int4 halves memory and data sent compared with int8, but Cortex-M cores have no 4-bit arithmetic, so compute runs at int8 speed and energy.

Wearable MCU STM32H7A3ZI

“(BLE)” = the chip has a built-in BLE radio. “CPU only” = the chip's neural-network accelerator is not used. MVP = SiLabs' matrix accelerator, which its measured result uses.

A MAC (multiply-accumulate, a × b + c) is the basic operation of a neural-network layer. MACs per clock cycle is how many the core completes per cycle on real neural-network code.

BLE wearable → phone

BLE sends data in short bursts called connection events, one per connection interval, each carrying a few packets. Six packets per event is a common phone setting; at 2 Mbps and 30 ms up to 21 fit (1.37 Mbps; Nordic reports 1.32 Mbps for a documented LE 2M setup), so state which one your phone supports. Lost packets are sent again, because BLE acknowledges every packet. Transmit-power levels follow Ixana's nRF52-class table (check that your chip supports the level). Higher power only adds energy here, because range is not modelled.

Wi-R Ixana YR23, wearable → on-body hub

Wi-R has no acknowledgements, so a lost packet cannot be sent again on request. Below 100% delivery every packet is sent k times, with k the fewest copies that deliver the whole feature map with the target probability (losses assumed independent). Each Wi-R packet also carries a 40-byte header. The blank wake-up energy uses the legacy model, 2 × sleep power × wake time, which is an assumption rather than a measurement. Transmit power is assumed not to change with data rate.

Idle time & battery

Every part of the wearable is counted over the whole time between inferences. The BLE link keeps its connection (one empty connection event every keep-alive interval) and Wi-R sleeps, including while the MCU computes. A longer keep-alive interval (or BLE peripheral latency) lowers that cost; disconnecting between inferences would add a reconnection cost that is not modelled. “Awake only to hand each packet over” charges a wake-up per packet plus the time to move the data over the MCU-to-radio link, and sleeps otherwise. With DMA, check that the chosen sleep mode keeps DMA and the link running; DMA and bus energy are not modelled. Energy at the battery = energy at the chip supplies ÷ conversion efficiency + regulator quiescent draw. Battery life counts the MCU and radio only, not the sensors or analog front end.

How this is calculated
  • Model sizes. We ran each model once in PyTorch on one input and recorded, for every layer, its output size, its MAC count and its weight count (script: feature_map_tx_energy.py, PyTorch 2.10). The weights are random, which does not change any size or count; accuracy is not modelled.
  • Compute energy = MACs × energy per MAC. Energy per MAC is the chip's MLPerf Tiny measurement (an industry benchmark that measures whole-chip energy on small neural networks) where one exists; otherwise run current × supply voltage ÷ MACs per cycle, from the datasheet. Example: 10.5 M MACs × 478 pJ = 5.01 mJ on the STM32H7A3ZI. Floating point costs more than int8: ×2.2 on Cortex-M7 (MLPerf Tiny on the STM32H7A3 shows 1.9–3.9×), ×2.7 on M4/M33 (≈2.7× measured on a PSoC 6 M4), ×20 on M0+/M23 (no floating-point unit; rough estimate), and ×1.1 (fp16) or ×2.2 (fp32) on M55/M85. Only MACs are counted; softmax, normalisation and activation functions also take time, so compute is a lower bound. MLPerf Tiny uses small CNNs, so transformer models (LSM, SensorLM, SleepFM) may run less efficiently than this.
  • Overlapping windows. For AttX every option, the raw input included, sends data for the full 10-s window, although successive windows overlap by 6 s. Reusing the overlap at the phone is not modelled. This makes streaming no cheaper than it is: a phone that kept the previous 6 s would need only the new 4 s.
  • Fits the MCU? The weights of every layer up to the split must fit in Flash, and the working memory must fit in SRAM, both at the chosen number format. Working memory = the largest single layer's input + output. We assume element-wise layers overwrite their input, attention runs one head at a time, and SleepFM's tokenizer runs one 5-s window at a time. Skip-connection buffers and the input buffer are not counted, so this check is optimistic.
  • BLE, all-at-once mode. Data goes out in connection events, one every connection interval, each carrying up to the set number of packets, and never more than fit the interval. Each packet carries the max payload minus 7 B of L2CAP and ATT-notification headers: 251 − 7 = 244 B. In each exchange the radio receives the phone's empty packet, waits 150 µs and transmits its own packet; 150 µs separate exchanges, with no gap after the last one. Every packet carries 10 B on air besides its payload at 1 Mbps and 11 B at 2 Mbps (2-byte preamble), without encryption. Each event adds 130 µs of ramp-up and 100 µs of post-processing, and the radio sleeps for the rest of the interval. The last event carries only the packets and bytes that are left. The transfer ends when the last packet has been sent; the phone acknowledges it at the next connection event. Example: 4096 B ÷ 244 B = 17 packets; at 6 per event that is 3 events, and the last packet ends 66.7 ms after the first event starts. Transmit and receive currents come from the selected MCU's built-in radio, or from Ixana's nRF52-class values if it has none; transmit current scales with transmit power using Ixana's table.
  • Wi-R, all-at-once mode. The radio wakes from deep sleep once per transfer (2 ms), settles (16 µs), then sends packets back to back, each with up to 255 B of data plus a 40-B header, with no acknowledgements. Gaps, if set, come only between packets. Example: 4096 B in 17 packets = (4096 + 17 × 40) B × 8 ÷ 5 Mbps = 7.64 ms, plus 2.02 ms of wake-up and settling = 9.66 ms. Transmit power is assumed not to change with data rate. The default wake-up energy is the legacy model's 2 × sleep power × wake time (12.3 nJ for a deep-sleep wake), an assumption rather than a measurement; enter your own value in the Wi-R settings to test it.
  • Lost packets. BLE acknowledges every packet and sends lost ones again, so attempts = packets ÷ share delivered. Wi-R cannot resend on request, so each packet is sent k times, with k the fewest copies for which the whole feature map of M packets arrives with the target probability, [1 − (1 − p)k]M, where p is the single-attempt delivery. Losses are assumed independent; bursts of loss would be worse. 100% delivery is the ideal no-loss baseline.
  • Packet count = bytes ÷ data per packet, rounded up; the last packet carries only what is left.
  • Total energy per inference. Every part of the wearable is counted over the whole time between inferences, so its states add up to that time. MCU: computing, then each transfer (awake throughout, awake only to hand packets to the radio, or asleep while DMA does it, per the setting), asleep for the rest. BLE radio: the transfer's connection events, then one empty connection event every keep-alive interval for all other time, including while the MCU computes (a long-run average). Wi-R: the transfer, then sleep for all other time, including while the MCU computes. These are energies at the chip supplies. The battery feeds them through a regulator, so energy at the battery = supply energy ÷ conversion efficiency (100% by default, so no loss, as in the paper; about 85% is typical for a regulator) + the regulator's quiescent draw. A uniform efficiency changes every total by the same factor, so it does not change which split is best. Battery life = battery capacity × battery voltage ÷ average power at the battery. It counts the MCU and radio only, not the sensors or analog front end, so it is a compute-and-communication estimate, not a device lifetime.
  • Latency and feasibility. Latency = wearable compute time + waiting for the first BLE connection event (per the setting) + transfer time; the phone's part is not included. A split is feasible if its latency fits the time between inferences (4 s for AttX, 30 s for EnsembleSleepNet, 5 h for LSM, 5 min for SleepFM, 1 day for SensorLM), every connection event fits its interval, the MCU can feed the radio fast enough, and Wi-R meets its delivery target. Infeasible splits are flagged, get no battery life and are never picked as best.
  • Simplifications. Compute and sending happen one after the other. For an MCU with a built-in BLE radio, its datasheet transmit and receive currents are used and a separate radio's 1.9 µA sleep current is not added, since the chip's sleep current covers it; the 3-mA radio-processing current and the timing still come from Ixana's nRF52-class model and may overlap with the MCU's own run current. The memory check is a lower bound until runtime buffers are counted. EnsembleSleepNet's spectrogram (STFT) is costed at the neural-network energy per MAC, though an optimised FFT library (CMSIS-DSP) may be cheaper.
  • One-packet-per-interval mode (not shown in Settings; the mode control in the page's code) follows the scheduling of Ixana's online calculator: one packet per interval on both radios, with the same packet exchange as above and Wi-R energy counted over a full wake-send-sleep cycle by default. A Wi-R interval shorter than wake-up + settle + send time is flagged as impossible.
  • Sources.
